# Limitations and Residual Risk

## What the policy can and cannot accomplish

The proposed Synthetic Media Use and Governance Policy can make approved university work more transparent, documented, and accountable. It cannot guarantee that synthetic media will remain correctly labeled, that every person will follow the rules, or that outside audiences will interpret a disclosure as intended. This limitations analysis identifies where the policy is most likely to fail and what risk remains after its controls are applied.

## Dependence on good faith

The policy depends on project owners accurately classifying their work, disclosing the tools they used, preserving scripts, reporting technical constraints, and seeking review. A well-intentioned employee may decide that a change is only a minor edit when it actually changes meaning. Another may reuse an approved synthetic narrator for a new purpose without realizing that approval was project-specific. A manager under deadline pressure may treat the review process as optional.

A bad-faith employee or contractor can bypass the policy more directly. Consumer generation tools are widely available, and a person can create content through a personal account or device. The policy can restrict official systems and impose consequences, but it cannot prevent every unauthorized act before it occurs.

Training, access controls, audits, and consequences reduce this risk. They do not remove the human judgment on which the policy relies. That reliance is not unique to synthetic media; governance of confidential information, financial systems, and public communications also depends partly on people following rules. The honest response is to make violations harder, more visible, and more costly rather than pretending they are impossible.

## Consent cannot eliminate downstream misuse

Written consent improves agency and establishes boundaries, but it does not control every copy after publication. A person may approve a synthetic voice for one orientation video and later regret the decision. The office can stop future distribution and remove copies from its own channels, but it may not be able to retrieve downloaded, archived, quoted, or reposted versions.

Consent may also be formally valid but socially pressured. An employee, student worker, athlete, or junior staff member may believe that refusing a senior request will harm a professional relationship. The policy requires voluntary and specific consent, yet a form cannot fully measure power imbalance.

The policy responds by limiting duration, requiring a separate release, explaining copying risk, and allowing revocation. Residual risk remains because consent is a process rather than a permanent transfer of control. Reviewers must examine the circumstances, not merely confirm that a signature exists.

## Disclosure can be separated from the content

The strongest disclosure can still be cropped, muted, mistranslated, omitted from an excerpt, or lost when a platform changes its interface. Audio is particularly vulnerable because an opening disclosure can be removed with a simple trim. A persistent video label can be covered or cropped. Captions and descriptions can become detached from downloaded files.

Repeated and multi-channel disclosure improves durability, but it also creates practical limits. Repeating a spoken warning too often can reduce usability and accessibility. A label placed over the entire image may interfere with its purpose. The policy therefore balances clarity and burden rather than making disclosure physically inseparable from meaning.

Residual risk remains that an altered copy will look official. A public verification page, consistent file naming, known institutional channels, and rapid correction can help audiences locate the official version. None can guarantee that every recipient will see the correction before acting.

## Provenance is useful but incomplete

Content Credentials can make origin and alteration history tamper-evident, but adoption is not universal. A creation tool may not generate a credential. A platform may not display it. Metadata can be removed, and the absence of a credential does not prove that content is false. Durable watermark or fingerprint approaches can improve recovery but may also fail after transformation or may not be available in the selected workflow.

Most importantly, provenance authenticates a chain of assertions; it does not determine whether the approved script was accurate. An institution can cryptographically sign a false or misleading statement. Trust therefore still depends on the signer, the verification process, and the sources behind the message.

The policy pairs Content Credentials with internal records, human ownership, and fact-checking. Residual risk remains when audiences do not have compatible tools, do not inspect provenance, or trust a credential from an unfamiliar or compromised signer.

## Detection will produce errors and contradictions

Task 6 provides a direct example. The detector labeled the Fish Audio clip as 1 percent real, but it gave the ElevenLabs clip a 95 percent authentic headline while its written analysis described AI-like features. A person reading only the score and a person reading only the explanation could reach opposite conclusions.

Detectors can fail because generators change, audio is compressed, clips are short, voices and languages differ, or post-processing removes learned signals. Combining multiple detectors may provide more evidence, but correlated systems can repeat the same mistake. Human listeners are also unreliable when high-quality synthetic speech is delivered without warning.

The policy limits detectors to screening and investigation. Residual risk remains that a real file will be flagged or a synthetic file will pass. There is no detector threshold that can replace provenance, source confirmation, context, and judgment.

## Review may become slow or ceremonial

Requiring several reviewers for high-risk projects can delay legitimate work. Under time pressure, teams may attempt to classify a project at a lower risk level. Reviewers may approve a request because the correct people have signed rather than because they have examined the script, consent, data handling, and expected distribution.

The policy cannot guarantee thoughtful review. Checklists can improve consistency but may turn difficult ethical decisions into box-ticking. Periodic audits and written rationales can reveal patterns, while a clear refusal rule gives reviewers authority to stop a project. Residual risk remains that organizational incentives reward speed and novelty more than caution.

## Vendors and platforms remain outside direct control

The university can select approved vendors and negotiate contractual requirements, but it cannot control every provider's model updates, retention practices, safety settings, or financial stability. A vendor may change its terms or remove a feature used for disclosure or provenance. A platform may re-encode media, alter label placement, or enforce policies inconsistently.

The vendor review and internal archive reduce dependence on outside systems. They do not eliminate it. The university may have to stop using a tool or withdraw content when a provider changes in a way that undermines the original approval.

## The policy cannot bind outside actors

The policy governs the communications office and its vendors. It does not govern students acting personally, unrelated organizations, anonymous accounts, or hostile actors. Someone outside the university can clone a leader's voice, copy university branding, and distribute a fabricated message without ever entering the institutional workflow.

The office can publish verified originals, monitor reports, request removal, preserve evidence, and correct false claims. It cannot guarantee prevention or complete deletion. Public education about authenticated channels is therefore part of resilience even though it is not a technical control.

## New tools will create unanticipated uses

The policy is based on current forms of generated audio, video, images, avatars, and provenance. Future systems may generate interactive representatives, personalized live calls, or multimodal content that does not fit existing categories. A six-month review cycle reduces the period of mismatch but cannot anticipate every capability.

The minimum-necessary-use principle and escalation rule provide a way to handle uncertainty. Residual risk remains that a new use will be deployed before its consequences are understood or that old language will be stretched to justify it.

## Enforcement has limits

Tool-access removal, retraining, contract remedies, and disciplinary action can deter violations inside the organization. Enforcement after publication cannot undo all harm. A false emergency message, unauthorized endorsement, or copied voice may affect an audience before the office responds.

Incident response therefore focuses on speed, evidence preservation, notification, correction, and learning. It cannot restore the exact level of trust that existed before the incident. This is one reason the policy categorically prohibits some uses instead of trying to manage every risk through disclosure and approval.

## Overall residual risk

After the policy is applied, the remaining risks include unauthorized creation, stripped disclosures, incomplete provenance, detector error, coerced or outdated consent, review failure, vendor changes, platform inconsistency, external impersonation, and irreversible redistribution.

These limitations do not make the policy pointless. The policy creates a defensible boundary between assistance and impersonation, assigns accountable humans, records consent, requires resilient disclosure, preserves evidence, and establishes a response when controls fail. Its value is not a guarantee that synthetic media will never cause harm. Its value is that the organization will make fewer unexamined uses, will refuse the most dangerous ones, and will be able to explain and improve its decisions.
